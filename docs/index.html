Catching the 1 in 600

Out of 600 card payments, only one is a fraud. This study shows how well a model can find it, what it costs in false alarms, and which decision it leaves to management.

The short version

The problem

A needle in a haystack

Out of 600 card payments, one is a fraud. A check that let everything through would be right 99.83% of the time and would stop no fraud at all.

The result

Most frauds caught, very few false alarms

The model gives each payment a risk score. On 56,746 payments it had never seen, it catches 71 of 95 frauds (75%) with only 2 false alarms. Fraud losses fall from €14,766 to €4,062, which is 72% less.

The decision

How many alerts are you willing to handle?

Where to set the alert level is a management choice, not a technical one. If a false alarm costs you €5, the standard setting is enough. If it costs only €1, a more sensitive setting pays off: 82 frauds caught instead of 71, at the price of 302 false alarms instead of 2.

Keep in mind: these results come from a public dataset of two days of card payments, with only 95 frauds in the test. It is a demonstration of method, not a promise of performance. Before any roll-out, the analysis should be repeated on your own data, with your real costs, and checked over time.

Why “99.83% correct” proves nothing

Out of 283,726 payments, only 473 are frauds. A system that approved every payment would be right 99.83% of the time and would stop none of the fraud. So the share of correct answers is a poor yardstick here. Two concrete questions matter instead: how many frauds do we catch, and how many of our alerts are justified?

For specialists

Accuracy is replaced by recall, precision and PR-AUC (area under the precision–recall curve), the right family of metrics for rare events. The decision threshold is treated as a business choice rather than a default of 0.5.

  3. 3

    Prepare the amount and the time of day, and set aside 20% of payments that the model never sees, to grade it fairly at the end.

  4. 4

What the data shows

283,726 payments over about 48 hours. Each payment is described by 30 pieces of information: 28 are anonymised by the bank to protect customers, plus the amount and the time of day. Nothing is missing.

Fraud hides in small amounts

The typical fraud is €9.82, against €22.00 for a normal payment, and 36% of frauds are €1 or less (versus 11% of normal payments). That looks like thieves testing a stolen card, though the data cannot confirm it. Either way, the amount alone cannot tell the two apart.

Fraud peaks in the small hours

Around hour 2 there are 145 frauds per 10,000 payments, and 104 around hour 4, against 16.7 on average. Caution: the data gives time elapsed since the start, not a clock time, so the hour of day is an approximation, and 473 frauds over two days is a small sample.

A few anonymised signals carry most of the information

Each bar shows how strongly a piece of information is linked to fraud (its correlation). V17, V14, V12 and V10 stand out, yet none reaches 0.31 (out of 1): no single signal is enough, which is why a model that combines them is needed.

Frauds stand apart, but not cleanly

On the four signals most linked to fraud, frauds sit in a different zone from normal payments, but the two groups partly overlap. That is where models able to spot combinations have an edge.

Four approaches, one fair test

Each approach learns from the same 226,980 payments (378 frauds), then is graded on 56,746 payments it has never seen (95 frauds). The final model was chosen before that test, never on it, so the grade is honest. The approaches range from logistic regression (the simplest) to random forest and XGBoost (more powerful, built from many small decision trees). Each is graded with the same score, from 0 to 1 (higher is better).

For specialists

Score = PR-AUC. Models are compared by 5-fold stratified cross-validation, then tuned with a randomized search (3-fold) on PR-AUC. Class weighting is used to handle the imbalance, and the intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals (300 resamples) on the test PR-AUC.

Which model finds frauds best?

XGBoost gets the best grade: 0.820 out of 1 (95% range 0.756–0.900), against 0.801 for the random forest and 0.674 for logistic regression. Each curve shows the trade-off: the more frauds we want to catch (horizontal axis), the lower the share of justified alerts (vertical axis). The dashed line is pure chance (0.17%). With only 95 frauds in the test, the ranges of the top two models overlap: the gap between them is not proven and should be read with caution.

How many alerts to accept? A management decision

The model gives each payment a risk score between 0 and 1. You then choose the score from which an alert is raised: this is the “threshold”. A low threshold catches more frauds but raises more false alarms (honest customers disturbed, analysts kept busy); a high threshold does the opposite. Move the slider to see the best choice for what a false alarm costs you. Every figure is measured on payments the model has never seen.

For specialists

Thresholds are always chosen on out-of-fold predictions of the training set, then applied once to the untouched test set. Three options are compared: the default 0.5, the F1-optimal threshold, and the cost-optimal threshold.

Assumptions: a fraud that goes undetected costs the payment amount; a false alarm costs a flat fee (analysis time, customer annoyance). €5 is a starting value I chose, not a measurement: replace it with your real costs.

How to read the small tables: columns are what the model announces, rows are reality. Bottom right: frauds caught. Top right: false alarms. Bottom left: frauds missed.

The test holds only 95 frauds (€14,766 in total), so these figures would change noticeably with another split. They illustrate the trade-off, they do not guarantee future performance.

Why the model raises an alert

A model that blocks payments must be able to justify itself. Two views: the weight of each piece of information (what the model relies on most) and a method called SHAP, which measures, for each payment, how much every piece of information raised or lowered the risk score. Computed on a test sample (95 frauds plus 250 normal payments drawn at random).

In which direction each signal pushes the score

Each dot is a payment. Right: the score moves towards fraud. Left: it moves away. Colour tells whether the signal's value is low or high.

Why was this payment flagged (or missed)?

Three individual decisions. Bars show the six signals that weigh most: red pushes towards fraud, blue towards a normal payment.

Signals V1 to V28 are anonymised (the public dataset hides their meaning to protect customers): we know which one weighed, not what it stands for. In a company, the same analysis would run on named information (country, type of merchant, usual amount…), which makes a decision explainable to a customer or an auditor.

Key takeaways

1

The share of correct answers is misleading

Approving everything scores 99.83% and catches no fraud. What counts is the share of frauds caught and the share of justified alerts.

2

Tree-based models spot more

Random forest and XGBoost reach 0.801 and 0.820, against 0.674 for logistic regression (the simplest): fraud follows complex patterns, not one simple dividing line.

3

The alert level is a management decision

At €1 per false alarm the best threshold drops to 0.005: 82 frauds caught instead of 71, but 302 false alarms instead of 2. From about €5 upwards the result changes little from one threshold to the next. The right setting depends on what a false alarm and a missed fraud really cost you.

4

A decision must be explainable

When a model blocks a real customer's payment, being able to say why is part of the product.

Limits and next steps

Test over time

Here, learning and testing both draw from the same two days, which is optimistic. In production the model learns from the past and must predict the future: that is what to simulate next (time-based validation).

Try other rebalancing methods

Here frauds are given more weight while learning. It could be compared with creating artificial frauds (SMOTE).

Make scores easier to read

Calibrate the model so that a score of 0.8 really means “about 8 frauds out of 10”, which makes it easier to keep a stable threshold over time.

More frauds to validate

Only 95 frauds in the test: treat the results as an order of magnitude. More data, or repeated splits, would narrow the ranges.

Plain-language glossary

The technical terms used in this report, in everyday words.

False alarm (false positive)
A normal payment flagged as fraud. Cost: analyst time and an annoyed customer.
Undetected fraud (false negative)
A fraud that goes through. Cost: the amount of the payment.
Frauds caught (recall)
Out of all real frauds, the share the model flags.
Justified alerts (precision)
Out of all alerts raised, the share that are real frauds.
Threshold
The risk score from which an alert is raised. Lower means more alerts, higher means fewer.
Overall quality score (PR-AUC)
A grade from 0 to 1 that sums up the trade-off between frauds caught and justified alerts. Better suited than “share of correct answers” when fraud is rare.
Cross-validation
Learning and grading several times on different slices of the training data, to check that a result is not a coincidence.
95% range
The zone in which the true grade very likely lies. A wide range means few cases, so more uncertainty.
SHAP
A method that tells, for one payment, how much each piece of information raised or lowered the risk score.
Anonymised data (PCA)
The 28 signals V1 to V28 are mathematical combinations of the original data, scrambled to protect customers.
Class imbalance
When one case is far rarer than the other (here 1 fraud in 600). It makes naive measures misleading.
Settings (hyperparameters)
Adjustments made before learning, such as how deep the decision trees can go. They are tuned by trial and error.
